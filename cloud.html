Cloud computing.

What it means to run software and store data over the internet instead of on your own hardware — and the tradeoffs that come with it.

What is it Types Advantages Disadvantages

Overview

What is Cloud Computing

Cloud computing means having the ability to store and access data and programs over the internet instead of on a hard drive. This means businesses of any size can harness powerful software and IT infrastructure to become bigger, leaner, and more agile, competing with much larger companies. Unlike traditional hardware and software, cloud computing helps businesses stay at the forefront of technology without having to make large investments in purchasing, maintaining, and servicing equipment themselves.

Categories

Types of Cloud Computing

SaaS — Software as a Service

Lets users access software applications without needing to download, install, or store that software and its components on their own devices. Most SaaS is subscription-based (monthly or annual), giving users seamless features without needing hardware or dealing with updates and maintenance themselves.

IaaS — Infrastructure as a Service

Covers the fundamental building blocks of computing that can be rented: physical or virtual servers, storage, and networking. It's attractive to companies that want to build applications from the ground up and control nearly every element themselves — but it requires the technical skill to orchestrate services at that level.

PaaS — Platform as a Service

Provides developers with an easy-to-use platform to build their own software, web applications, or other projects. Businesses use PaaS to create proprietary apps and programs without needing their own servers or testing environments.

The upside

Advantages of Cloud Computing

  • Minimal time investment to get up and running — no equipment to set up or maintain. Providers handle maintenance, security, and software updates.
  • Every person in your company can access data from devices they already use, anywhere they need it — keeping teams up to speed and enabling faster collaboration.
  • No hardware or equipment to buy, no software to install, and no licensing fees.
  • Providers offer redundant storage (often across multiple data centers), stronger security, and faster recovery.
  • You only pay for what you use — add resources as your team grows, or scale back when you don't need them.
  • Security is handled by your provider, both online and physically at their data centers.
The tradeoffs

Disadvantages of Cloud Computing

  • Risk of data loss or theft.
  • Every file — image, audio, video, etc. — lives on the cloud and is accessed over an internet connection. Without good connectivity, you simply can't reach your data; there's no offline fallback.
  • Cloud infrastructure is entirely owned, managed, and monitored by the service provider, which means users have less control over how services actually run.
  • Providers implement strong security standards, but adopting cloud technology means sending your organization's sensitive information to a third party — which carries some risk of it being compromised by hackers.
  • Vendor lock-in — companies using one vendor's cloud services can find it hard to switch to another, due to high switching costs, large volumes of data that are difficult to migrate, and other complexities. This can force companies to keep using a provider even when it no longer suits their operational needs.
