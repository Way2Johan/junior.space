Government action on drugs in India.

Several ministries share responsibility for the drug problem, working along three broad lines: cutting off supply, reducing demand, and limiting harm.

The framework

Three approaches, three sets of ministries

As in most countries, India's response splits into three strands, each handled by different parts of government.

Supply reduction

Reducing the availability of illicit drugs by enforcing drug laws. Handled largely by the Department of Revenue under the Ministry of Finance, the Narcotics Control Bureau under Home Affairs, and various central and state agencies.

Demand reduction

Reducing how many people want drugs, through prevention, treatment and rehabilitation. The Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment leads here, and has run a scheme for the prevention of alcohol and substance abuse since 1985–86, delivered largely through NGOs.

Harm reduction

In India this mostly means preventing HIV among people who inject drugs. Strategies are run by the National AIDS Control Organization under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare.

1988 onwards

The Drug De-addiction Programme

Through the 1980s, rising drug use prompted a push to treat substance use disorders through the health system rather than only through social welfare schemes. The division agreed was that the Health Ministry would handle treatment while the Social Justice Ministry handled prevention and rehabilitation.

Following a Cabinet subcommittee's recommendation, the Drug De-addiction Programme launched in 1988. It set up 30-bed de-addiction centres in six premier hospitals for inpatient treatment, then expanded in 1992–93 into medical colleges and district hospitals across the states.

Funding was split: the central government paid one-time setup costs, while states covered staff, supplies and running costs — an arrangement reflecting that health is a shared responsibility under the Constitution. North-eastern states received additional recurring assistance of up to ₹200,000 per centre per year.

Around 122 centres were established this way, 43 of them in the north-east. The centre at AIIMS New Delhi was designated the National Drug Dependence Treatment Centre in 2002, with an expanded role advising the programme nationally.

A small programme

The programme is run by a section within the Health Ministry, headed by a Director reporting to a Joint Secretary. Most states have no equivalent office, and no senior official at the Union Health Ministry works on addiction treatment full-time. The approved budget for the five years from 2012 to 2017 was ₹151 crore.

Capacity building

Training general doctors

Because the programme could only run a limited number of services directly, its main effort went into training. India has far more people with substance use disorders than it has psychiatrists, so the strategy was short in-service courses for general duty medical officers already employed by state health services.

Curricula were developed at national expert workshops in 1988 and 2003. Courses initially ran three weeks, then shortened to 14, 10 and finally 7 days in response to participant feedback. More than 1,000 doctors have been trained.

Trainees consistently rated the courses highly, which makes sense given that MBBS study includes almost no teaching on substance use. Pre- and post-training test scores confirmed real gains in knowledge. Courses worked best when lectures were combined with live case demonstrations and time in outpatient clinics.

The persistent difficulty has been getting state governments to release doctors for training at all, given their clinical workload. And there is no clear evidence yet that training translates into better services back at the hospital.

Data

The Drug Abuse Monitoring System

Established to track patterns of drug use and the profile of people seeking treatment, this system has collected annual data from government de-addiction centres since 2007. Each centre reports clinical and demographic details for new patients on a one-page form, collated and analysed centrally.

Compliance has been poor. Analysis covering 2007 to 2013 found that 57% of centres took part at all, and only 39% sent data regularly. Patient load varied enormously between centres, and on average each participating centre saw very few new patients — meaning that while the country lacks treatment capacity, the services that do exist attract almost nobody.

Evaluation

What the reviews found

Evaluations in 2002 and 2006–07 visited or surveyed centres across several states. Most were providing some treatment, but few were working well. The recurring problems:

  • No state funding for services at most centres, apart from the north-eastern states receiving central grants.
  • Low priority given to addiction treatment by hospital authorities.
  • Trained doctors often absent, with no dedicated nurses or counsellors either.
  • Very few patients accessing services, and those who did were rarely retained in treatment.
  • Inadequate record keeping, and medicines needed for treatment usually unavailable.
  • Psychosocial interventions frequently not offered, and community-based activity largely absent.

A further evaluation in 2007 collected three years of outcome data from 44 busier centres and reached the same conclusion. The model — inpatient care, set up centrally, sustained by states — had worked only partially.

The redesign

Drug Treatment Clinics

Experience in India and elsewhere showed that most patients can be treated as outpatients, with only some needing admission. So NDDTC at AIIMS designed a new scheme, approved for the 2012–17 plan period, establishing Drug Treatment Clinics inside existing government hospitals.

The important change was funding. Rather than depending on state budgets, the central government now pays directly for staff salaries and medicines. Each clinic has a nodal officer from the hospital plus three contractual staff — a doctor, a nurse and a counsellor.

Clinics provide both psychosocial and drug treatment, with all medication free: short-term withdrawal management and long-term therapy including opioid agonists, antagonists and anti-craving medicines for alcohol use disorder. Patients needing other services are referred within the hospital or to NGOs.

By December 2016, 14 clinics were running across Delhi, Haryana, Maharashtra, Manipur, Punjab, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh, with 25–30 planned by the end of 2017 and a longer-term target of at least 100. The initiative won the British Medical Journal South Asia Award for 2016 in the non-communicable disease category.
